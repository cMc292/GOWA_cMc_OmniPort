# S and J: a review before starting (2026-11-16)

S is a stub that runs for a kept EDGE job in place of the job module; J is the job manager's load path as native code
(`wwspm`). [What is left to gain](2026-11-11-next-candidates.md) put them together at -19 to -35 SPU-ms a flip and +4% to
+10% in the fight, weeks of work, high risk. This review reads the code and the measurements again before any of it is
written. No code changed.

## In short

- **Gate 0, measured the same day ([below](#gate-0-what-an-spu-microsecond-is-worth)):** 5 µs more on every kept job costs the
  fight 1.7% ± 0.6% on six cores and 3.3% ± 0.5% on four. S+J, ~25 SPU-ms a flip if their microseconds hold, would give a
  four-core CPU ~+9% (27 flips/s to ~30) and a six-core one +2-4%. It pays where the CPU is small.
- **S has less room than its name says.** The output stage is native since phase 2b (`native/spu_edge_out.c`), and a kept
  job already leaves out the transforms, unpacking's arrays and its vertexes' PUT. What a stub can still take is the job
  module's flat code between the key and the output: ~5 µs of a position hit and ~10 of a lit one, by the traces of
  [the worker review](2026-11-06-worker-time-review.md). The job manager's calls have to stay.
- **J is the larger part.** The job manager is ~15.6 µs of every job. ~4.7 of it is the wait for the transfers, which no
  rewrite takes away; the rest is ~350 labels of flat lifted code and the runtime under them.
- **At today's counts** (2,487 jobs a frame, 92.6% kept, 2026-11-12): S -11 to -23 SPU-ms a frame, J about -12 for EDGE
  and some for the other job workloads. Together -23 to -35, as the table had it.
- **What a frame gains for that is the open question.** Four measurements put an EDGE millisecond at 0.18-0.57% of the
  frame rate, and D, -3.5 ms, showed no gain at all in eight runs. So the first step is a measurement, not code.
- **Neither touches issue #9's drops** in Kirra and the temple: those are the backend's kept vertexes filled again
  ([kept fills](2026-11-16-kept-fills.md)), not SPU time.

## What changed since the estimates

| | then | now |
|---|---|---|
| jobs with a kept result | ~88% of EDGE's time (2026-11-06) | 92.6% of jobs (2026-11-12) |
| a kept job's vertexes | PUT to the ring | 16 bytes; the backend draws a resident copy (`GOWA_EDGE_RESIDENT`) |
| skinned jobs | run every stage | kept, skinned in the vertex pass (K) |
| velocity jobs | dear | cheaper (A); their late binormals left out tried and not kept (D) |
| the output stage | lifted | native (2b) |
| jobs a frame in the fight | ~1,700 | 2,487 |

## S, a stub for kept jobs

**What a kept job must still do,** from the [output contract](2026-11-04-edge-output-contract.md) and the
[host reference](2026-11-04-edge-host-reference.md), in this order:

- the job manager's calls, as the module makes them: `0x2FD8` three times, `0x17B8`, `0x17C0` (tag `0x1E`, the job's own
  GETs) and `0x17E0` (the next job's prefetch starts there, and the job manager's interrupt handler carries it on at the
  job's interrupt checks);
- the 16-byte record of descriptor word 22, twice, to `0x10710`, and the record the GPU's pass takes;
- the ring: the line's `GETLLAR` (`0x41A0`), and when word 28 is set the program block's `PUT` into the second ring
  between a `GETLLAR` and a `PUTLLUC`; then the main ring's allocation;
- the PUTs: the index list, the vertex block's first 16 bytes (resident), and the hole with its methods, fenced;
- `r3` on return: the job's run time in decrementer ticks.

**Still open in the contract:** `0x67C0`, `0x61B0`, `0x9A60`, the 16-byte record's fields and descriptor words 19, 20 and
33-35. The kept lit jobs work today because the module still runs those; a stub has to do each of them itself.

**The way:** at the key (`gowa_edge_memo_9a40`), on a hit, a native routine makes the calls and the writes above in their
order and returns to the module's end (`0xAE44`), so the module's driver is left out. Position hits first: no program, no
light record, and 31% of the jobs. Then lit hits.

**The check:** `GOWA_EDGE_MEMO=2` already compares a result with what the stages make. A stub needs its own: run it, run
the module on a copy of the local store, and compare the local store, the PUTs, the ring line and the records, as the
output stage's native was checked. The replay's strict mode (`gowa.py replay`, a `--jobcap` build) gives the exact jobs.

**The risk:** a missed write shows as wrong geometry or a chain that waits forever, on a tag or a ring position.

## J, the job manager's load path native

**What it is.** `wwspm` is 198 lifted functions (`spu/wwspm`, 5,265 lines); 78 of them run in EDGE's jobs, the largest at
0.8% of the samples. Its share is 9.4-11.5% of EDGE's samples, and much of the runtime's 32-40% (channels, MFC, interrupts,
coherency) is under it. Tag waits: `0x2370` 4.7 µs (the transfers happen there), `0x2EA0` 1.0, `0x2540` 0.7, `0x35E0` 0.9.
The two 40-pass loops at `0xA60` and `0xAAC` are native already (P).

**The ways:**
1. The load path native, as J was meant: the DMA list's GET, the elements' `GETL`, the tag waits, the interrupt handler's
   part, the polls of the SPURS workload state. Weeks, and the protocol with the SPURS kernel has to stay exact.
2. The runtime paths the job manager goes through most, first (R's third pass, aimed at it): days, and it helps every
   lifted module.

**The check:** `wwspm` is one module with one protocol, and the replay's strict mode runs it with the jobs.

**The risk:** the rare hang at level load, `256 identical transfers to ea=0x70039D00 -- halting the SPU`, is in the job
manager's poll at `0x25E4`. Still not explained (2026-11-06, 2026-11-08). A native load path changes that code's timing.

**It helps more than EDGE:** the job manager is 4% of RenJobSet's samples, 6% of audio's, 2% of w6's, and those
milliseconds sit on the chain.

## What it could give

- **S:** ~2,300 kept jobs a frame at 5-10 µs: -11 to -23 SPU-ms.
- **J:** 2,487 EDGE jobs at ~5 µs: about -12 SPU-ms, and some of the other workloads'.
- **A millisecond's worth:** 0.18% (the slope test, 2026-11-06), 0.32 (the motion blur draws off), 0.57 (A, runs with heat
  in them). D's -3.5 ms gave none, its eight runs ordered ABBA and BAAB, every run without it below every run with it.
- **So** +4% to +6% at the slope's 0.18, up to +10% at the table's upper end. A gain under ~3% cannot be shown by a few runs
  of the fight on this laptop.
- **Who sees it.** The fight runs 29-31.5 flips/s uncapped on the test laptop (six cores) against the game's 30: S+J is
  headroom for that 30 on such CPUs, and for frame generation's. A CPU with more cores spreads the SPU work wider and holds
  30 there already.

## The order

1. **A measurement first** (one or two days, three runs of the fight):
   - **The slope probe:** a calibrated delay on every kept job, switched on and off every 10 s within one run, so that the
     laptop's heat moves both halves alike. One run at +5 µs, one at +10. The frame rate's change per µs is what S's and
     J's microseconds are worth.
   - **The job-time probe** on today's build: per class, the module's µs and the job manager's.
   - If 5 µs on every kept job is worth under ~1.5%, S+J would not show in the fight: stop there.
2. **S for position hits** (about a week): the stub, its check mode, the replay. Measured with the same switching.
3. **S for lit hits.**
4. **J,** the job manager's runtime paths first, then the load path native if the probe says it pays.
5. **Every step:** the replay's strict mode, `GOWA_EDGE_MEMO=2`, a game run through the gate and the fight, and a level
   load for the hang.

## How much to trust it

- The µs inside a job are from 2026-11-06's marks; the output stage has gone native since, and the class costs are from
  2026-11-11. Step 1's probe brings both up to date.
- The estimates of this kind have run high by a factor of four (the worker review), and flat code has paid badly when
  made native (the glue): natives pay in loops.
- J's "native at half" is a guess.

## Gate 0: what an SPU microsecond is worth

Three runs of the fight, 2026-11-16, on the Ryzen 5 4600H (six cores, twelve threads) on mains power: the bench's route
(Profile 1's Continue Game on the test saves, `logs/benchhdd0`), the settings of `build/ascension-r.ini` (FSR 3.1 at 1.5,
SMAA, borderless, the overlay, Disable MLAA and Disable Shadows) with `--set graphics.frame_generation=off --set
display.vsync=off --set cheats.damage_taken=0`. Port `5b06ded` with a temporary probe, runtime `062200a2`. Logs in
`logs/sj-gate0-d5`, `logs/sj-gate0-d10`, `logs/sj-gate0-c4d5`.

### First: the bench's fight was held by frame generation

The benches of [the Kit's profile](2026-11-14-kit-pgo.md) and of 2026-11-15 sat at 30.2-30.3 flips/s in every window,
put down to the title's own lock. All their logs say `frame generation: a 60.00 Hz display, 30.00 flips a second`: the
settings' frame generation held them. Without it the fight runs ~30-31 flips/s on the average, ~25 in its heavy moments
and ~36 in its light ones. `gowa.py bench` now says which hold it was, and how to measure past frame generation.

### The probe

- **A delay on every kept job, in turns.** `GOWA_EDGE_HIT_DELAY=<µs>:<s>` spun that long at the key of every job with a
  result, off and on by turns within the run, so both halves share the scene and the heat. Every half second was logged
  with the flips and the kept jobs so far; a turn's first half second, while the frames in flight change over, was left
  out.
- **The scene's own swing.** The fight's load goes from ~1,300 to ~2,600 kept jobs a flip and back every few seconds, and
  the frame time with it, from 25 ms to 45. Turns of 5 s fell in step with it in the first run (on turns 35 flips/s, off
  turns 28, then the other way round). So turns of 3 s, and each turn's frame time fitted to its kept jobs a flip and
  the on/off. That gives ±0.2-0.35 ms (±0.5-1%) in one run, where two runs of one build differ by 1.3%.
- **Four cores** were `GOWA_CPU_MASK=0xFF`: the process on four cores, eight threads (kept as a bench setting).

### The results

| run | CPU | delay | turns in the fight | SPU-ms added a flip | frame time | frame rate | of the delay's time, on the frame |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | six cores | 5 µs | 15 of 5 s | +10.0 | +0.54 ± 0.19 ms | -1.7% ± 0.6% | 5% ± 2% |
| 2 | six cores | 10 µs | 53 of 3 s | +20.3 | +1.98 ± 0.35 ms | -5.8% ± 1.0% | 10% ± 2% |
| 3 | four cores | 5 µs | 53 of 3 s | +10.5 | +1.25 ± 0.17 ms | -3.3% ± 0.5% | 12% ± 2% |

- **Without the delay** (the off turns): 32.5 and 33.2 ms a flip on six cores (30.8 and 30.1 flips/s), 36.4 on four (27.4).
- **The scene's load does not change the share:** under and over 2,000 kept jobs a flip, run 2 has 8.9% and 9.6%, run 3
  13.4% and 12.1%.
- **The threads,** a window at 150 s:

  | | the workers (7) | main | frame clock | RSX render | the process |
  |---|---|---|---|---|---|
  | six cores | 556% | 96% | 56% | 49% | 861% of 1,200% |
  | four cores | 517% | 72% | 53% | 44% | 762% of 800% |

### What it says

- **Six cores:** the CPU is not full, and the frame takes 5% of a small delay's time and 10% of a double one. Time taken
  away is worth less still: the first microseconds go to slack. 0.17% a SPU-ms, near the 0.18 of the slope test of
  2026-11-06.
- **Four cores:** the CPU is full, and an eighth of the delay's time is on the frame: what eight busy threads give back
  for work taken off them, so time taken away should be worth the same. 0.31% a SPU-ms.
- **S+J,** if their microseconds hold (S 5 µs a position hit and 10 a lit one, ~14 SPU-ms a flip; J ~5 µs on each of
  ~2,100 jobs, ~10.5 ms): ~25 SPU-ms a flip.
  - Four cores: 12% of it, 3 ms of 36.4: **~+9%**, 27.4 flips/s to ~30.
  - Six cores: under 5% of it: **+2-4%**, the lower end more likely. With frame generation on (FSR 3's default), such a CPU
    holds 30 through most of the fight already; the gain would show in its heavy moments.
  - Estimates of this kind have run high by up to four times: +2% to +9% on four cores, +1% to +4% on six.
- **The bar** (5 µs worth 1.5% or more) is passed on four cores and only just on six. S+J is for the smaller CPUs.
- **On a full CPU every thread's time counts the same,** about an eighth of it on four cores. The other candidates
  ([U, V, R](2026-11-11-next-candidates.md)) take days where S+J takes weeks, and are worth measuring with the same
  yardstick.

### If it goes ahead

1. **S for position hits,** with a switch that turns the stub on and off by turns within one run, measured on four cores
   with the same fit: there a 1% gain shows in one run. Alone ~+1.3% there (~800 hits × 5 µs); what it builds (the stub,
   its check, the replay) lit hits take over.
2. **S for lit hits:** ~+3% on four cores.
3. **J:** ~+4% on four cores, with the level-load hang to watch.

Not done: the job-time probe inside a job (the module's µs against the job manager's). Its numbers come with step 1,
which has to map the same path.

## Step 1: U, kept unskinned jobs without unpacking

Decided with the user after Gate 0: U first, as S's cheapest part, then S. Done the same day (`GOWA_EDGE_UNPACK`,
`native/spu_edge_memo.c`).

- **What it does.** An unskinned job with a result (GOWA_EDGE_RIGID, the velocity jobs with it) still unpacked its
  stream, only for the case that the result is given up later and 0xF538 and 0xBBC8 run on the SPU after all. Now it
  leaves the decoding out, as a skinned result does: the natives of `0x75BD10` and `0x764D10` still fill the attribute
  list and the context, those of `0x30310` and `0x304A0` run one pass. A revoke gives the arrays back from the result
  (`er_give`) before the calls left out run: where the job's 0xF538 reads them, or for an array it has not reached,
  where the context (`0x107A0`) has it, the position's at `+0x5C`, the normal's `+0x64`, the tangent's `+0x68`.
- **Why the result is enough.** It keeps the arrays as 0xF538 reads them. `0x304A0`, the largest unpacking (5.97 ms a
  flip in the fight), writes exactly three arrays, two vertexes a pass at the pool's words, which it also writes into
  the context: the same three.
- **The check** (`GOWA_EDGE_UNPACK=2`): every result's arrays left unpacked, given back at the packing, which then runs
  on the SPU and is compared with what the CPU makes of the result. In the fight: 7,036,849 jobs (5,776,232 unskinned,
  1,260,616 velocity), 2.0 billion vertexes, no position word and no normal or tangent byte different, and no array
  away from the context's place.
- **Measured** with the new in-run switch: `GOWA_TURNS=3` turns it on and off by turns of 3 s, `scripts/turns.py`
  makes the fit of Gate 0. One run each, the fight from 69 to 229 s after the load, 53 turns:

  | CPU | frame time | frame rate | light turns | heavy turns |
  |---|---|---|---|---|
  | four cores | -0.77 ± 0.20 ms | +2.05% ± 0.52% | +2.4% | +1.9% |
  | six cores | -0.32 ± 0.20 ms | +0.94% ± 0.60% | +1.8% | +0.5% |

  As Gate 0 had it: ~5.5 ms a flip of the kept jobs' unpacking, an eighth of it on four cores, 0.7 ms.

## Where a kept job's time goes now

The sampler on the EDGE workers after U (`PS3_SAMPLE=1`, `PS3_SAMPLE_THREAD=spurs pm`, the fight on six cores, w14-16,
182,000 samples):

| | of EDGE's samples |
|---|---|
| the runtime (`spu_*`, `mfc_*`) | 31.1% |
| EDGE's natives | 21.3% |
| the job module, lifted | 13.6% |
| outside the executable (waits) | 10.7% |
| the job manager, lifted | 10.6% |
| the pieces, lifted | 4.7% |

- **The largest single function was the normals loop,** `gowa_edge_normals_a950`, 5.9%: the velocity jobs' binormals
  (below).
- **The job module's own code is spread thin:** `0x88B0` 1.3%, `0x51D0` 1.1%, `0xBFB8` 0.8%, `0xA880` 0.7%, `0x9990` and
  `0xE550` 0.6% each, the rest below. A stub takes only what is on a hit's path and not the job manager's.
- **The runtime's calls between lifted functions** are about 13%: `spu_overlay_note_get` 3.7%, `spu_indirect_branch`
  2.9%, `spu_lifted_lookup` 2.2% (and its full form 0.8%), the drain calls 2.2%, `spu_smc_microstep` 1.3%. They are in
  every workload, not only EDGE (the candidates' R).

## Step 2, first part: a velocity job's binormals (D again)

A velocity job calls `0xA880` twice (back at `0x54F8` and `0x5AEC`), and its loop at `0xA950` makes the binormals, which
only a lighting stage reads. A velocity job has none, and its output piece packs the position, the normal, the tangent
and the previous position. With the lighting on the GPU the first call was left out and then run late, the second ran.
This is [D](2026-11-11-next-candidates.md), tried on 2026-11-12 and not kept: right, but eight runs in ABBA and BAAB
order came out slower with it, every one, unexplained.

- **Now** (`GOWA_EDGE_VEL_BINORM`, on): the hook at `0xA928` leaves both calls out for a velocity job, unskinned or
  skinned, and neither runs late.
- **The check,** `GOWA_EDGE_VEL=2`: 1,386,867 velocity jobs, 448 million vertexes, no position word and no normal or
  tangent byte different. The normals loop went from 3,355,817 calls a run to 17,831, and with them 48 million of its
  exact half-ops (the slow form near-zero vectors take).
- **Measured by turns** (`GOWA_TURNS=3:vbn`, U on throughout), one run each:

  | CPU | frame time | frame rate | light turns | heavy turns |
  |---|---|---|---|---|
  | four cores | -1.18 ± 0.23 ms | +3.26% ± 0.63% | +4.0% | +3.2% |
  | six cores | -0.82 ± 0.16 ms | +2.42% ± 0.48% | +1.2% | +2.9% |

- **So the runs of 2026-11-12 were wrong,** the heat and the run order, which turns within one run share. It also saves
  more than the 3.5 SPU-ms a frame the probe's clock gave it: those exact half-ops are dear.
